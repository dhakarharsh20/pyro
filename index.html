INDUSTRY APPLICATIONS

What are you measuring, and where?

WHAT YOU'RE MEASURING

Billet surface at the rolling mill exit. Ladle shell temperature. Reheating furnace interior. Continuous caster tundish. TMT quench zone exit temperature.

WHY CONTACT FAILS HERE

You can't place a contact sensor on steel moving at line speed. Ladle shells exceed sensor survival temperature. Furnace interiors need readings without disturbing the atmosphere.

THE KEY CHALLENGE

Scale and steam between the pyrometer and the billet distort the reading. Emissivity varies as the surface oxidises through the rolling process. Ladle shells need to be scanned continuously — one cold spot means refractory failure and a furnace shutdown.

RECOMMENDED SERIES

A Series or E Series for fixed mount. Portable P Series for spot-checking.

SCROLL 06 — TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE

WANT THE FULL TECHNICAL PICTURE?

Explore technical specifications, optical principles, emissivity physics, environmental installation guidance, and integration standards.

A pyrometer is a thermal sensing tool that consists of a wide range of devices in order to read the temperature of an object without direct contact. The pyrometer’s optical system is able to capture the electromagnetic radiation emitted by the object. Once passed through the optical system, the infrared rays reach a transducer, which then converts the rays into a small electrical signal.

The pyrometer equipment also includes electronic components capable of amplifying the signal, filtering out unwanted noise, linearizing it and correcting the signal depending on some parameters. The final measurement may then be displayed on a computer screen, panel, or recorded with the help of a datalogger.

Spectral range & why it matters

The spectral range of a pyrometer is defined as the range of infrared wavelengths used to determine the temperature, usually expressed in micrometres (µm), such as 0.8–1.1 µm, 1.6 µm, 3–5 µm, or 8–14 µm. The importance of spectral range is because different materials emit infrared radiation in a different manner at different wavelengths.

The right spectral range helps to achieve better measurement accuracy and decreases errors introduced by emissivity, flames, smoke, gases, glass, or different materials that may be located between the pyrometer and the object. Short wavelengths of a pyrometer are suitable for high-temperature metals while longer wavelengths may be used for cold objects and non-metallic surfaces. Thus, a proper spectral range ensures accurate temperature measurements.

Emissivity plays an important role in making the temperature measurement accurate by means of a pyrometer. Emissivity refers to the capability of a surface to emit IR radiation compared to an ideal blackbody, with values ranging from 0 to 1. Different materials, surface treatments, oxidation states, and temperatures can give rise to various emissivity values. An incorrect emissivity setting can lead to considerable errors in measurement, especially for shiny or reflecting metals. It is important to set the pyrometer according to the type of material in question, the surface condition, and the working temperature, while checking the emissivity value every time the processing conditions change.

  • Oxidised surfaces → generally have higher and more stable emissivity, making them easier to measure accurately.
  • Polished metals → have low emissivity and are highly sensitive to surface condition and reflections; short-wavelength, two-colour measurement, or careful emissivity calibration may be required.
  • Glass → around 5.0–5.14 µm is commonly preferred for high-temperature glass surface measurement. 8–14 µm can also measure the surface of glass at lower temperatures; it does not normally measure through ordinary glass.
  • Plastics and rubber → 8–14 µm works well for many opaque plastics and rubber because they generally have high emissivity. Thin or semi-transparent plastic films may require a different spectral range.

The pyrometer is often the easy part. The installation determines whether you get a useful reading. Common mistakes: mounting too close to the target (small field of view misses the target), no air purge in dusty environments (window fouls, reading drifts), incorrect sighting tube length (reads the tube wall temperature, not the target).
